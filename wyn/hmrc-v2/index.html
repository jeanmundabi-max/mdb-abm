The hinge

A framework sets what a supplier may charge at most. A call-off sets what you actually pay. A renewal is the only moment that number moves.

After it renews it is settled for the whole of the next term, and no amount of good management moves it. That is why this page carries a date rather than a season.

What it costs to do nothing

Nothing, on the day. That is what makes it easy. The cost is that a number agreed on 30 November 2026 is then the number for the whole next term, and the next opportunity to look at it is a term away.

On a call-off running at £116,746,575 a year, a difference of one percent is £1,167,466 a year. Two percent is £2,334,932. This page is not claiming either figure applies to you. It is showing what the arithmetic of one percent looks like at this scale, so that reading a free document before a signature is easy to justify.

And the arithmetic is the same for the other 15 contracts, which between them come to £77,556,727 a year. Those renewals are spread across the next 7 months, so this is not a single moment. It is a sequence of them, and the first one is the biggest.

There is a second cost and it is harder to see. A renewal agreed without a comparison is a renewal nobody can defend afterwards. If a public accounts committee, an internal auditor or a journalist later sets your call-off beside another department's, the useful answer is not that the price was reasonable. It is that it was checked, and here is what it was checked against. That document exists either before the signature or it does not exist at all.

What is actually in the document

What arrives

A single PDF. One page per contract, and a summary sheet in front of it. For each of your 16 live contracts it names the supplier, the published value, the term, the annual value, the end date, and what the same supplier is charging other departments on comparable published work. Where there is no comparator it says so rather than estimating.

What does not arrive

A savings figure with your name on it. Nobody outside your building knows your consumption, your scope, or what sits behind a framework call-off, and a number produced without those is theatre. The document says where you sit and stops.

What happens next, if anything

Nothing automatic. No follow up sequence, no calendar link. If the document is useful you will know who sent it. If it is not, that is the end and it cost you an email address.

What happens after it arrives

Nothing automatic. There is no follow up sequence, no calendar link in the footer, and nobody telephones your switchboard. The document is the whole of the first exchange, and if it is not useful it has cost you an email address and ten minutes.

If it is useful, the next question is not commercial. It is whether the differences it shows are scope or price, and that is a question your own commercial team can put to the supplier without anybody else in the room. A department that reads the document and then negotiates its own call-off better has had the entire benefit of this at no cost, and that is a legitimate outcome.

The commercial arrangement only enters if you want the negotiation work itself: the specialists, the drafted emails, the vendor knowledge. That step starts with your commercial team and your information assurance team, not with a form, and it may never happen. Nothing on this page is designed to make that step feel inevitable, because at a government department it is not.

And if the answer is no. There is no second document, no sequence and no retargeting. This page was built for one organisation and it is shown to nobody else.
